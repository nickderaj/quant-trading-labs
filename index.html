Key terms everything on this page uses only the definitions below — click any term

›Value at Risk (VaR)

An estimate of how much a position could lose over a given period, at a given confidence level. "1% VaR of 3%" means: on a typical day, there is roughly a 1% chance the position loses more than 3%. It is a loss threshold, not a worst case — by construction, it is meant to be crossed occasionally.

›Expected Shortfall (ES)

The average loss on the days that fall past VaR's threshold. If 1% VaR is 3%, 1% ES is the average size of the loss on the worst 1% of days — always a bigger number than VaR, and a better sense of how bad the bad days actually get.

›Violation (breach of a single day)

A day where the real loss was bigger than that day's VaR estimate. Some violations are normal and expected — a 1% VaR should be crossed on roughly 1 day in 100. What the monitor checks is whether they happen at the right rate, and whether they cluster together in time instead of spreading out.

›Breach rate

The share of days in a product's trailing history where a violation actually happened, compared against the expected rate (1% or 2.5%). Close to expected is healthy. Well above expected, or one long streak of violations in a row, is not — see "calibration status" below.

›Calibration status: OK / WARN / BREACH

The monitor's overall verdict for a product, from a battery of statistical tests run on its trailing history. OK: the tests pass. WARN: violations are running somewhat hot or clustered, worth watching. BREACH: the tests fail outright — the VaR/ES figures above may be understating real risk until this is investigated.

›Failure mode

When a product breaches or warns, this says which specific test failed, because different failures call for different fixes:

  • coverage — violations are simply too frequent overall; the volatility estimate is probably too low.
  • clustering — the overall frequency is fine, but violations bunch together in time; the model is reacting too slowly to changing conditions.
  • shape — violations happen at a normal rate, but the worst losses are bigger than the model's tail shape predicts.
  • coverage + clustering — both problems at once, the most serious case.
›σt (today's volatility)

A causal, day-by-day estimate of how volatile a product is right now, computed only from past returns (an exponentially-weighted moving average, so today's number never uses today's return). This is what scales the VaR/ES figures up or down from day to day.

›Dependence mode

Three different assumptions about how products move together in the tails, used to simulate the book's combined risk. The Gaussian copula assumes products become independent in extreme moves, which understates real joint risk — it is shown for comparison, not because it is recommended. Empirical and Student-t (t-copula) both let products crash together, which is closer to what actually happens.

›Horizon (1D / 5D / 10D)

VaR/ES scaled from the 1-day figure using the square root of the number of days. This assumes returns are independent from day to day — a simplifying assumption, not a model separately fit to 5- or 10-day moves.

›Distribution family (GED, Student-t, skew-t, …)

The mathematical shape fit to a product's day-to-day returns, chosen per product from a small zoo of candidates because plain bell curves (normal) tend to understate how often big moves happen. Student-t and GED allow fatter tails than normal; Hansen skew-t, Johnson SU and NIG also allow the tails to be different sizes on the downside vs. upside. This is what the badge next to each ticker (e.g. "GED family") refers to — hover it for that product's specific shape.

Scope. This tool reports estimated loss potential (VaR / Expected Shortfall) and model calibration only. It computes no alpha, no positions, no Sharpe ratio, no backtest, and no equity curve, and produces no recommendation or trading signal anywhere on this page. It answers "how much could this position lose," never "should I hold it."
